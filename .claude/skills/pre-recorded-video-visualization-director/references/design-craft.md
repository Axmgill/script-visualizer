# Design and Craft: How Strong Motion Designers and Editors Work

Read this file before Phase 5, and keep it open through Phases 8–10. It is the craft layer: the
process strong designers run, and the standards their work is judged against.

## The premise

**There are no recipes in this file.** Every rule is a lens for judgment, not a filter to apply. The
moment a rule is used to decide *what* a video looks like, it has become a template — which is the
failure this skill exists to avoid.

What separates top-tier work is not more techniques. It is:

1. **Generation before selection** — three real directions, not one safe one.
2. **A visual system** — decisions made once, consistently, rather than per-shot invention.
3. **Restraint** — what was removed, and why.
4. **Critique** — the work is attacked before the client sees it.

Amateurs decorate. Professionals edit. The question is never "what can I add here" but "what earns
its place here".

---

**Precedence when the files seem to disagree.** `mindmap-previz.md` owns the truth of the
argument: what the script actually claims, which lines are abstract, and which anchor carries each
one. This file owns the look and the process: how those claims become a system and a set of frames.
`visual-plan.md` owns sequence: scenes, shots and storyboard timing. If a design idea needs a claim
the script does not make, the mind map wins and the idea is cut. If a shot needs a colour or type
treatment this system does not allow, this file wins and the shot is redesigned.

## Part 1 — The process

Eight gates. Each produces an artifact that can be argued with. None of it is linear in practice, but
the order of *commitment* is.

### Gate 0 — Read for tension, not topic

Do not summarise the script. Find the argument and what it costs the viewer to accept it.

- What is being claimed?
- What does the viewer have to give up to accept it?
- Where is the resistance, and who is holding it?
- What does the writer care about that they haven't said out loud?

**Artifact:** one sentence of tension, in the form *"The viewer believes X; the video has to make
them feel Y."*

> Bad: "A video about Thomas." Good: "The viewer thinks doubt is weakness; the video has to make
> doubt feel like loyalty with better questions."

**Gate question:** can you say the tension in one sentence without using the word "journey"?

### Gate 1 — Research and reference, specifically

Reference is not inspiration. It is evidence that a look is achievable and a way to argue about
direction.

Collect 6–10 references across four buckets, plus one anti-reference:

| Bucket | What you take from it |
|---|---|
| Composition | framing, negative space, focal hierarchy, aspect and crop |
| Colour and light | value structure, palette roles, contrast, texture of light |
| Type | family, scale, measure, case, how type sits with image |
| Motion and texture | pacing, easing, how elements enter and leave, grain, imperfection |
| Anti-reference | the one thing this must not look like, and why |

**Banned reference language:** "clean", "modern", "premium", "bold", "cinematic", "minimalist". These
are adjectives, not references. Name the thing: a photographer, a film, a title sequence, a piece of
print, a painting, a specific channel's look.

**Artifact:** 6–10 named references, each with one line: *what I am taking from it*.

**Gate question:** could two different people build the same look from this list? If not, the list is
not specific enough.

### Gate 2 — Three territories

Three distinct visual directions. Not three shades of the same idea.

Each territory is one sentence: **"The video looks like X, because the script says Y."** The second
half is not optional — it is what makes the direction an argument rather than a mood.

Rules:

- **Mutually exclusive.** If two territories could coexist in one video, they are one territory.
- **Executable.** Each must be buildable with the assets that exist or can be obtained.
- **Each carries a risk.** State what could go wrong in each: too cold, too cute, too expensive,
  too slow to animate, misread by the audience.
- **Kill test:** drop all three into a different script. If they still work, they are generic
  house styles, not responses to this material.

**Artifact:** three territories, each with thesis, what the viewer sees, what could go wrong, and a
recommendation with a reason.

**Gate question:** can you defend the chosen one against the other two in a sentence?

### Gate 3 — Commit, and turn the territory into a system

A territory becomes a system the moment it answers these, once, for the whole piece:

| System | Decide |
|---|---|
| Grid and ratio | e.g. 12-column, 3:2, 8pt baseline; how type and image relate to it |
| Palette roles | ground, ink, accent, alert — with a value range, not a mood |
| Type scale | one family for speech, one for quotation (max two); a fixed scale ratio (1.2–1.333) |
| Shape language | sharp vs rounded, geometric vs hand-made, consistent stroke weight |
| Texture | none, grain, paper, scan lines, real material — and where it is allowed to appear |
| Motion grammar | what may move, what may not, how things enter and leave |
| Sound palette | what kinds of sound are allowed, and what silence is for |

**Rules that hold across every piece:**

- One accent colour. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
- Value before hue: a piece that works in greyscale will work in colour.
- Two type families maximum; three weights maximum.
- The system must survive the ugliest frame in the video.

**Artifact:** the system as a compact spec an editor could follow without asking questions.

### Gate 4 — Style frames (4–6)

A style frame is a *design*, not a screenshot request. It fixes composition, light, colour, type and
texture for one key moment, and it must survive being looked at on its own.

For each frame, specify:

- **Name** (what moment it is)
- **What is in the frame**, in order of visual weight (primary, secondary, tertiary)
- **Negative space** — how much, and where
- **Framing and lens feel** — wide, normal, macro, flat graphic
- **Light** — source, direction, hardness, falloff
- **Palette roles used** — which of ground/ink/accent/alert appear
- **Type present** — family, scale step, case, placement
- **Texture** — where and how much
- **What moves next** — the frame is a still, but it is never the whole story

Rules:

- **Four to six frames**, chosen from different *functions* (hook, proof, turn, payoff), not four
  variations of the prettiest beat.
- **They must read as a series:** shared grid, palette and type. Any two frames side by side should
  look like the same film.
- **Include the hardest frame**, not just the showcase one. If the hardest frame cannot be designed,
  the territory is wrong.
- **Describe it well enough that another designer could rebuild it** in Figma or Photoshop without
  asking what you meant.

**Gate question:** does each frame have exactly one focal point, and does the type survive being
shrunk to a phone?

### Gate 5 — Motion tests

Motion is designed, not discovered in the timeline. Before animation, decide:

- **Timing per beat** — how long each idea needs, in frames.
- **Easing vocabulary** — where the piece is mechanical, where it is human, where it snaps.
- **What holds** — the shots that don't move. Holds are how the fast moments get their speed.
- **Transitions** — only those that carry meaning: match cut, object carried across, type morph,
  hard cut, sound-led. A list of transitions is not a style.
- **Kinetic type rules** — how words arrive: whole line, word by word, mask reveal, hard swap. One
  rule per piece, not one per sentence.
- **Sound sync points** — where sound lands and what it is punctuating.

**Rule:** every move needs a stated reason — *reveal, connect, emphasise, or pace*. If the reason is
"it looks cool", it is decoration, and the critique will find it.

### Gate 6 — Build

- Name things so a stranger can edit them: layers, comps, assets, versions.
- Design animation-ready: separate elements, no baked text, handles on every move, extra frame at
  each end.
- Keep the system file (palette, type, grid) as a first-class asset, not a screenshot.
- Build the ugliest frame first. It sets the floor for the piece.

### Gate 7 — Critique in three passes

Never review structure, craft and polish at the same time — you will fix the wrong thing.

**Pass A — Structure (does it argue?)**
Does each beat earn the next? Is anything redundant? Is the strongest idea at the start or buried at
1:40? What would you cut if you lost 15 seconds?

**Pass B — Craft (does it look designed?)**
One focal point per frame? Value structure legible in greyscale? Palette roles consistent? Type
hierarchy respected? Negative space deliberate rather than leftover?

**Pass C — Polish (does it feel finished?)**
Cuts on the beat or deliberately off it? Any frame that reads as default software? Audio pops, level
jumps, caption drift? Edges, handles, off-by-one-frame moves?

**Five tests to run on every piece:**

1. **Mute test** — does the picture still tell the story without sound? If not, the visuals are
   subtitles for audio.
2. **Squint test** — squint at any frame: is the focal point still obvious?
3. **Stranger test** — someone who hasn't read the script: can they say what the video argues after
   one watch?
4. **Delete test** — remove each graphic. If the video is unchanged, it was decoration.
5. **Swap test** — put the visual in a different video. If it still fits, it was never about this
   script.

## Part 2 — Craft standards

### Composition

- **One focal point per frame.** Three levels of hierarchy maximum: primary, secondary, background.
- **Figure-ground:** the subject must separate from the field by value, colour, focus or motion —
  never by adding a stroke around it.
- **Negative space is a material.** Decide its budget per frame ("70% empty, type in the lower
  third") rather than filling what's left.
- **Use a grid and a ratio.** 12-column, 3:2, golden section, or a fixed 8pt baseline — pick one and
  keep it, so the series holds together.
- **Series thinking:** design frames as a set. Consistency beats individual brilliance; one weak
  frame in a consistent series reads better than one brilliant frame in an inconsistent one.
- **Simplify before styling.** Reduce to the fewest shapes that still carry the meaning, then style.
  Complexity is easy; reduction is the craft.

### Colour

- **Roles, not moods:** ground, ink, accent, alert. Each has a job.
- **Value first.** Build the frame in greyscale; if it doesn't read, colour won't save it.
- **One accent**, used only where the meaning is.
- **Contrast is the tool.** Measure it: text on background should clear 4.5:1 for body, 3:1 for
  large type.
- **Colour can carry argument** (warm/cold, alive/dead, evidence/speculation) — but only if it is
  consistent enough that the viewer learns it.

### Typography

- **Two families maximum**, with a clear job split (e.g. serif for quoted source, sans for
  narration).
- **A fixed scale ratio** (1.2, 1.25, 1.333). Three to five sizes, no more.
- **Measure and leading:** 45–75 characters per line for reading text; tighter leading for display
  type, looser for long lines.
- **Type as image:** type can be the whole frame — but then it must be *designed* (scale, crop,
  rhythm, tension), not centred and left.
- **Never decorate type:** no shadows, glows, gradients or outlines unless the system says so.

### Motion

- **Weight:** things have mass. Ease in and out; almost nothing moves linearly.
- **Anticipation and overshoot** in small doses make motion feel physical.
- **Stagger** related elements; simultaneous arrival reads as a slide deck.
- **Holds create speed.** The fast moments only feel fast because something before them was still.
- **Match cuts** connect ideas: same shape, same position, same gesture across a cut.
- **Motivated movement only:** reveal, connect, emphasise, pace. Name the reason or cut the move.
- **Pattern breaks are currency.** Spend them twice per minute, not twice per second.

### Data visualisation

A chart is a designed object, not a widget. It gets the same treatment as a frame: one focal point,
one accent, a grid, and a reason.

- **The chart is chosen by the relationship, and the relationship comes from the sentence.** A move
  over time, a share of a whole, a ranking, a rate, a gap between two totals — each has shapes that
  show it honestly and shapes that flatter it. Start from the claim, not from a chart type you like.
- **If the sentence is only a figure, there is nothing to chart.** Set the number and hold it. A chart
  of a single quantity invents a comparison that the narration never made.
- **Direct labels beat a legend** whenever there is one series. Label the line, not the colour key.
- **The axis is the argument.** A truncated axis must be declared on screen. Nothing on a chart should
  be true only because of where zero was placed.
- **Annotate the moment the voice names**, not every point. One annotation beats ten gridlines.
- **Animate the change, not the furniture.** The number moving is meaning; bars building in one after
  another is decoration.
- **Keep the remainder visible.** If 1 of 10 is the story, the other 9 stay on screen.
- **Match the video's system.** Same type, same accent, same ground. A chart pasted from a template is
  visible from across the room.
- **Never fake evidence:** no invented data, no 3D, no decorative pies, no needle gauges, no growth
  arrows the script never claimed.

The test is the same one applied to every frame: **could this graphic be lifted into a different
video unchanged?** If yes, it was a template, not a response.

### Sound

- **Sound is punctuation**, not wallpaper. A cut can land on silence.
- **Design a palette:** room tone, material sounds, one musical texture. Nothing else.
- **No default risers**, whooshes or impacts. If a sound effect is there because the edit feels
  empty, the edit is empty.
- **Silence is a tool.** Use it where the argument needs weight.

## Part 3 — The critique rubric

Score each 1–5. Anything below 4 on **Idea** or **Truth** means rework, regardless of the total.

| Dimension | 5 looks like | 1 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Truth** | Every visual claim is in the script | Visuals assert things the script never said |
| **Idea** | The visual adds information the words don't carry | The visual restates the words |
| **Focal clarity** | One obvious entry point per frame | Everything competes |
| **Craft** | Grid, value, type and colour are deliberate | Default settings, uneven spacing, mixed styles |
| **Motion logic** | Every move has a reason and a rhythm | Movement as decoration |
| **Restraint** | What was cut is obvious | Everything that could be added, was |
| **Series** | Any two frames look like the same film | Each frame is its own experiment |
| **Execution** | Animation-ready, named, editable | Baked text, mystery layers, off-by-one moves |

### Anti-patterns, and why they fail

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| A dot grid because the line has a number | The visual is a template, not a response to the sentence |
| A lightbulb for "idea", gears for "process", a seedling for "growth" | Imported meaning the script never had |
| Slow-motion b-roll over an abstract line | Motion standing in for thought |
| Whoosh on every cut | Rhythm is being simulated, not designed |
| Three typefaces and four accent colours | No system, so nothing reads as intentional |
| Centred text on every frame | Composition by default |
| Teal-and-orange "cinematic" grade | A filter used as a style |
| Lower-third template with logo, line and shirred background | Software is doing the design |
| Kinetic type on every sentence | Emphasis loses all meaning when everything is emphasised |
| A chart for a sentence that is not comparing anything | A relationship was invented to justify a shape |
| Four numbers carried four different ways with four different looks | Variety standing in for a system |
| A chart and an object doing the same job in one frame | The carrier was never decided, so both were used |
| A truncated axis with no note | The chart wins the argument by lying about zero |

## Part 4 — Where this connects to the rest of the skill

- **Phase 4 (mind map)** produces the argument and the anchors. This file decides how it *looks*.
- **Phase 5** runs Gates 0–4 and outputs: tension, references, three territories, chosen system,
  style frames.
- **Phase 6 (emotional arc)** is the rhythm those frames sit inside.
- **Phase 7–9 (scenes, shots, storyboard)** expand the style frames into sequence; every storyboard
  frame must trace to a style frame or its system.
- **Phase 10 (motion graphics)** runs Gate 5.
- **Phase 16 (review)** runs Gate 7.
- **Phase 4's `stat` nodes and `abstract` nodes** are where Part 2's anti-patterns are most likely to
  appear. Check them against the rubric before anything is designed.

## Part 5 — Sources

The process above is distilled from how the craft is taught and practised, not invented here:

- **School of Motion — Design Kickstart:** contrast, positive/negative space, figure-ground,
  hierarchy, rhythm, grids and ratios, colour, value, type, shape language; boards designed as a
  series meant for motion.
- **School of Motion — Design Bootcamp:** style frames and storyboards for real client briefs.
- **School of Motion — Illustration for Motion (Sarah Beth Morgan):** interpret a script, develop
  client-facing options, simplify forms, use texture/depth, deliver animation-ready files, and design
  a key frame while thinking about the frames on either side of it.
- **AevyTV:** documentary-style motion graphics, kinetic typography, tracking graphics into real
  footage, retention pacing and pattern breaks, frame-by-frame breakdowns of reference edits, and
  mentor critique loops on real timelines.

Use these as standards to reach, not as material to copy.
